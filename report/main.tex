\begin{abstract}
\noindent We re-examine out-of-sample predictability of the US equity premium using a
fixed 240-month rolling-window design on data spanning February 1951 to December 2025.
The predictor set combines the fourteen Goyal--Welch (2008) macroeconomic variables
(with one to six monthly lags) and the fourteen technical indicators of
Neely, Rapach, Tu and Zhou (2014), which we reproduce from raw price and volume
data with a 99.98\% signal-month match to the published series. Against the
prevailing historical mean benchmark we evaluate $\ell_1$-penalized least
squares (LASSO and post-LASSO), Ridge, Elastic Net, principal components and
partial least squares regressions, and random forests, together with equal-weight
and constrained Granger--Ramanathan forecast combinations, at horizons
$h\in\{1,3,12\}$. Statistical accuracy is assessed with the Diebold--Mariano
(1995) test on squared and absolute errors,
and economic value with a binary switching strategy and a mean--variance
investor, both net of transaction costs. Our expanding-window comparison
uses 178 correctly timed predictor--target pairs before the January 1966
forecast, versus the 181-month initial window in the original study. Our
computed technical signals match the published series in 99.98\% of
signal-months. On the 1966--2011 sample, the three focal volume-rule
estimates are positive, but none of the six volume rules is significant at
5\%. On the extended sample, however,
no model beats the prevailing mean at any horizon: out-of-sample $R^2$ ranges
from $-15.49\%$ to $-0.38\%$, while hit rates are close to the benchmark.
At $h=1$, the LASSO switching strategy has lower terminal wealth and only
modestly smaller maximum drawdown than buy-and-hold, while mean--variance
utility differences are negative for all forecast models. At $h=1$ and
10 bps net, $\Delta U$ is $-0.39\%$ p.a. for LASSO and $-2.07\%$ for
post-LASSO. The corrected
results provide no evidence that these forecasts improve on the prevailing
mean in point-forecast accuracy or mean--variance utility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Whether the equity premium is predictable out-of-sample is one of the most
debated questions in empirical finance. Goyal and Welch (2008) showed that a
broad set of macroeconomic variables fails to beat the historical average in
real time, and Goyal, Welch and Zafirov (2024) reach the same verdict with a
greatly expanded predictor set. Against this backdrop, Neely, Rapach, Tu and
Zhou (2014) report that \emph{technical indicators}---moving average,
momentum and on-balance volume rules---do contain out-of-sample predictive
content for the premium on their 1966--2011 evaluation period, and Gu, Kelly
and Xiu (2020) document the promise of machine learning methods in asset
pricing more generally.

This paper asks a simple question: once the technical-indicator information
of Neely et al.\ (2014) is combined with the Goyal--Welch macro variables and
fed through modern regularized machine learning models, does anything beat
the prevailing historical mean on the extended 1951--2025 sample? We follow
a disciplined protocol: a fixed 240-month rolling window, a strict
no-lookahead alignment convention, the prevailing mean as the only benchmark,
Diebold--Mariano (1995) tests with HAC inference, and economic evaluation net
of transaction costs. To validate the data, we match the published technical
signals in 99.98\% of signal-months, then use a time-aligned expanding-window
bivariate design on the 1966--2011 sample. Its correctly timed predictor--
target pairs provide 178 initial observations, compared with 181 months in
the original design.

Three findings emerge. First, on the extended sample no model---LASSO,
post-LASSO, Ridge, Elastic Net, PCR, PLS, random forest, or forecast
combinations---beats the prevailing mean at any horizon; out-of-sample $R^2$
ranges from $-15.49\%$ to $-0.38\%$. Second, the three focal volume-rule
estimates are positive but do not reach 5\% significance on the 1966--2011
sample, and none of the six volume rules is significant at that level; the
three focal estimates are negative on the extension. Third, at $h=1$ the
LASSO switching strategy has lower terminal wealth than buy-and-hold and a
modestly smaller maximum drawdown, while forecast-based mean--variance
utility differences are negative. At $h=1$ and 10 bps net, $\Delta U$ is
$-0.39\%$ p.a. for LASSO and $-2.07\%$ for post-LASSO. The remainder of the
paper describes the data (Section~\ref{sec:data}), the methods
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), the results (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and
concludes in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources and sample}\label{sec:data:sources}
The target variable is the monthly log equity premium on the S\&P 500,
$y_t = 100\,(\ln(1+R^m_t) - \ln(1+R^f_t))$, where $R^m_t$ is the value-weighted
S\&P 500 return including dividends and $R^f_t$ the risk-free rate, taken from
the continuously updated Goyal--Welch--Zafirov (2024) dataset (monthly
observations 1871:01--2025:12). The premium is expressed in percent per month;
over the estimation sample its mean is 0.66\% with a standard deviation of
4.21\%. The fourteen macroeconomic predictors follow Goyal and Welch (2008):
dividend-price ratio, dividend yield, earnings-price ratio, dividend payout
ratio, stock variance, book-to-market, net equity expansion, Treasury bill
rate, long-term yield, long-term return, term spread, default yield spread,
default return spread, and inflation. Each macro predictor enters with one to
six monthly lags.

The fourteen technical indicators follow Neely et al.\ (2014): six moving
average rules $\mathrm{MA}(j,m)$ with $j\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $m\in\{9,12\}$
comparing short and long moving averages of the index level, two momentum
rules $\mathrm{MOM}(m)$, $m\in\{9,12\}$, and six on-balance volume rules
$\mathrm{VOL}(j,m)$ comparing moving averages of on-balance volume. Each
indicator is a binary signal (1 = hold equities). We compute the signals from
the raw index level and monthly S\&P 500 volume: the paper's own volume series
for 1950--2011 and monthly average daily volume from Yahoo Finance for
2012--2025 (the untruncated Yahoo series correlates 0.9999 with the paper
series on their 1985--2011 overlap, and the
volume-based signals are invariant to a constant volume rescaling). Against
the published signals of Neely et al.\ (2014) over their 1950:12--2011:12
sample, 12 of the 14 signals match exactly and the remaining two match in
99.9\% of months; the two mismatching months are documented data revisions.

\subsection{Alignment and sample period}\label{sec:data:align}
Row $t$ of the panel holds the premium \emph{earned} in month $t$ together
with predictors \emph{observable at the end of month $t$}. Macro lag columns
satisfy $x^{\mathrm{lag}\,k}_t = x_{t-k}$, $k=1,\dots,6$; technical signals at
row $t$ use price and volume data through month $t$ only, so no forecast uses
information unavailable at its origin. The sample is the first month in which
every column is non-missing (1951:02, driven by the twelve-month burn-in of
the volume rules) through 2025:12, giving $T=899$ monthly observations.
Table~\ref{tab:table1} reports summary statistics and augmented
Dickey--Fuller (BIC lag selection, intercept and trend) tests.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Rolling-window forecasting design}\label{sec:method:design}
All models are estimated on a fixed 240-month (20-year) rolling window and
produce an $h$-month-ahead forecast of the premium, $h\in\{1,3,12\}$. A
training observation pairs predictors $X_s$, observable at the end of month
$s$, with the premium earned in month $s+h$. At forecast origin $t$, training
pairs are restricted to outcomes observed by $t$; the window contains 240
valid pairs. The benchmark is the prevailing (historical) mean premium
computed through the forecast origin, the only admissible benchmark for this
exercise: OLS benchmark forecasts fail out-of-sample and are not used.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:method:models}
The predictor matrix is wide relative to the 240-month estimation window,
its columns are highly correlated (six lags of fourteen persistent macro
series plus fourteen signals that switch in clusters), and any true signal
is likely sparse and episodic. This is precisely the setting for which the
penalized and dimension-reduction estimators below are designed: the
$\ell_1$/$\ell_2$ penalties tame the collinearity, the component methods
condense the factor structure, and the random forest admits nonlinear
interactions and threshold effects that linear models cannot represent. We
estimate seven models within each window: (i) LASSO with the theoretical,
data-driven penalty of \texttt{hdm::rlasso} (no cross-validation required);
(ii) post-LASSO, i.e.\ OLS refit on the LASSO-selected support; (iii) Ridge
and (iv) Elastic Net ($\alpha=0.5$) with the penalty chosen by ten-fold
cross-validation \emph{inside the training window} (no information from the
test month is ever used); (v) principal components regression and (vi)
partial least squares with the number of components selected by
cross-validated PRESS (scaled inputs, at most 20 components); and (vii) a
random forest (500 trees, $m_{\mathrm{try}}=p/3$, minimum node size 5, fixed
seed, single-threaded for exact reproducibility). All models use the same
$98$-predictor matrix (84 macro-lag columns plus 14 technical signals); the
expanded Goyal--Welch--Zafirov (2024) predictors are held out as a
robustness variant. That variant adds only the eight expanded variables that
are observed monthly throughout 1951:02--2025:12 and are not constructed
with full-sample information (skvw, tail, rdsp, avgcor, dtoy, dtoat, wtexas,
lzrt), each with six lags, giving 146 predictors. We exclude fbm, ogap, sntm,
tchi and shtint because the data documentation describes them as full-sample
constructions, so lagging them would not remove look-ahead bias; twelve
further variables (e.g.\ cay, pce, eqis) are not available monthly; and csp,
vp, impvar, vrp, ndrbl, ygap, rsvix and disag start after or end before our
sample, so including them would shorten the common sample. The expanded
variant is therefore a partial, not exhaustive, use of the Goyal et al.\
(2024) predictor set.

\subsection{Forecast combinations}\label{sec:method:combos}
We combine the seven model forecasts with (i) the simple equal-weight average
and (ii) a constrained Granger--Ramanathan (1984) regression: at each origin
the weights solve $\min_w \|y-Fw\|^2$ subject to $\sum_i w_i = 1$ and
$w_i\ge0$, estimated only on past out-of-sample forecast errors known at that
origin (targets $\le$ origin). Before 60 usable observations accumulate,
equal weights are used.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:method:eval}
Statistical accuracy is measured by out-of-sample RMSE and MAE and the
Campbell--Thompson (2008) $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}} = 1 - \sum e_t^2 / \sum
e_{t,\mathrm{PM}}^2$ against the prevailing mean. Significance is tested with
the Diebold--Mariano (1995) statistic on squared and absolute error
differentials, with Newey--West long-run variance (lag $h-1$). Following the
course, we use the Diebold--Mariano test only and do not report the
Clark--West (2007) nested-model test. Economic value is assessed with two
strategies net of transaction costs (baseline 10 bps, sensitivity 5 and
20 bps). Each switch, including the initial equity purchase, is charged this
cost. We assume the investor trades an S\&P 500 ETF and Treasury bills; major
US brokers now charge zero online commission on both (e.g.\ Charles Schwab,
2026), and a one-cent quoted spread on a liquid S\&P 500 ETF priced in the
hundreds of dollars is well below 1 bp. The 10 bps baseline is therefore a
conservative all-in cost covering spread, slippage and price impact, and the
20 bps case reflects the higher trading costs of earlier decades: (i) a binary switching strategy that holds the S\&P 500 when the
$h$-month-ahead premium forecast is positive and three-month Treasury bills
otherwise, reporting terminal wealth, annualized Sharpe ratio, maximum
drawdown, and turnover; and (ii) a mean--variance investor with risk aversion
$\gamma=3$ who allocates $w_t = (1/\gamma)\,\hat y_t / \hat\sigma^2_t$ to
equities, clamped to $[0,1.5]$, where $\hat\sigma^2_t$ is realized variance
over the previous 60 months, measured using information available at the
forecast origin; the certainty-equivalent return gain
$\Delta U$ relative to the benchmark allocation is reported.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Statistical accuracy}\label{sec:results:accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:table2} reports out-of-sample RMSE, MAE, $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$,
hit rate, and the Diebold--Mariano $p$-values of every model
against the prevailing mean at each horizon, and
Figure~\ref{fig:r2oos} visualizes the $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ profile. The
central result is unambiguous: \emph{no model beats the prevailing mean}.
All nine forecast models have negative out-of-sample $R^2$ at each horizon.
At $h=1$, the prevailing-mean RMSE is 4.403; LASSO has the least negative
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ ($-1.06\%$), while random forest has the most negative
($-15.49\%$). Post-LASSO is closest to the benchmark at $h=3$ ($-0.38\%$)
and $h=12$ ($-0.52\%$). Across all horizons, the one-sided Diebold--Mariano
tests find no statistically significant evidence that a model improves on
the benchmark (minimum one-sided $p=0.677$). This
does not support the previous claim that models are significantly worse.
Hit rates range from 53.5\% to 60.1\%, compared with prevailing-mean rates
of 59.7\% to 59.9\%; thus, the direction metric is close to the benchmark
and is not uniformly lower. The full, technical-only and macro-only
predictor-set results are negative as well (Appendix~\ref{app:variants}).

\subsection{Replicating Neely et al.\ (2014)}\label{sec:results:neely}
To examine the individual technical signals, we estimate the expanding-window bivariate
predictive regressions of the one-month-ahead premium on one technical
signal at a time---on their 1966--2011 evaluation sample and on the
2012--2025 extension (Table~\ref{tab:table5}). With predictor signals dated before the return they forecast, the three
focal on-balance-volume estimates are VOL(2,9) $+0.19\%$ (one-sided

\subsection{Economic evaluation}\label{sec:results:econ}
Table~\ref{tab:table3} reports the two economic strategies net of transaction
costs, and Figure~\ref{fig:wealth} plots the switching-strategy wealth paths.
At $h=1$, the LASSO-driven switching strategy ends with \$232.7 per dollar
invested versus \$341.9 for buy-and-hold; its maximum drawdown is $-44.4\%$
versus $-50.2\%$, and its annualized Sharpe ratio is 0.767 versus 0.776.
These sample outcomes show lower terminal wealth and only a modestly smaller
drawdown for this specification. The mean--variance utility differences
relative to the prevailing-mean allocation are negative for every forecast
model at $h=1$ and at the reported fee levels. For example, at $h=1$ and
10 bps net of transaction costs, $\Delta U$ is $-0.39\%$ p.a. for LASSO,
$-2.07\%$ for post-LASSO, and $-0.99\%$ for the Granger--Ramanathan
combination. The results therefore do not support a claim of positive
mean--variance utility gains.

\subsection{Interpreting the sample comparisons}\label{sec:results:discussion}
Subperiod and predictor-set comparisons are descriptive and do not identify
why forecast performance differs. In particular, they cannot establish that
crisis months caused predictability, or that passive flows and faster
arbitrage explain any change over time. The corrected bivariate estimates
do not reproduce 5\% significance for the three focal volume signals on the
1966--2011 sample; the extension therefore does not establish that
previously reported predictability subsequently decayed. The macro-only and
technical-only comparisons in Appendix~\ref{app:variants} report
out-of-sample performance for those predictor sets; they do not establish
causal mechanisms. Figure~\ref{fig:subperiods} and
Table~\ref{tab:subperiods} report the underlying descriptive h=1 LASSO
subperiod estimates.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We apply a correctly time-aligned expanding-window bivariate design to the
Neely et al.\ (2014) sample, with 178 paired observations available before
the January 1966 forecast, and extend the analysis to 2025 with seven regularized
machine learning models and two forecast combinations. Our computed
technical signals match the published series in 99.98\% of months. The
three focal volume-rule estimates are positive but not significant at 5\%
in the original 1966--2011 evaluation period; none of the six volume rules
is significant at 5\%. On the extended sample, no
model beats the prevailing historical mean at any horizon. Since the corrected
analysis does not reproduce significance in the original period, these
results do not establish whether previously reported predictability decayed
after 2011.
At $h=1$ and 10 bps net of transaction costs, mean--variance $\Delta U$ is
$-0.39\%$ p.a. for LASSO and $-2.07\%$ for post-LASSO. The economic
strategies are evaluated separately from point-forecast accuracy; the
complete fee sensitivity results are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results:econ} and Table~\ref{tab:table3}.

Our study has clear limitations. It covers a single market (the US) with
monthly data; the rolling window length and lag structure are fixed rather
than selected; and the economic evaluation ignores short-sale constraints
beyond the $[0,1.5]$ weight bound and assumes the investor trades at the
index level. Future work could extend the exercise to international equity
markets, higher-frequency signals, or shrinkage schemes that combine the
prevailing mean directly with the model forecast---a natural response to the
persistent failure of point forecasts documented here.
