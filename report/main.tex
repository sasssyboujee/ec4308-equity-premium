\begin{abstract}
\noindent We ask whether regularized machine learning models, fed with the
fourteen Goyal--Welch macroeconomic predictors (one to six lags) and the
fourteen technical indicators of Neely et al.\ (2014), can forecast the
monthly simple US equity premium better than the prevailing historical mean.
Using a fixed 240-month rolling window on 1951:02--2025:12 data, we evaluate
seven models and two forecast combinations at horizons of 1, 3 and 12 months.
Only ridge regression attains a positive out-of-sample $R^2$ (0.12\% at
$h=1$ and 0.30\% at $h=3$), and none of the 54 one-sided Diebold--Mariano
tests rejects equal accuracy (smallest $p=0.23$). An adapted replication of
Neely et al.\ (2014) yields positive but insignificant single-signal gains
on 1966--2011 and uniformly negative ones on 2012--2025. Economically, a
ridge-driven switching strategy ends at \$499 per dollar versus \$342 for
buy-and-hold at $h=1$ net of 10 bps costs, but four of nine forecast
strategies trail buy-and-hold, most strategies lose at $h=12$, and these
gains are not tested for significance. Overall, we find no statistically
reliable improvement on the historical average.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Whether the equity premium is predictable out-of-sample matters both for
asset allocation and for asset pricing theory. Goyal and Welch (2008) showed
that a broad set of macroeconomic predictors fails to beat the prevailing
(historical) mean in real time, and Goyal, Welch and Zafirov (2024) reach a
similar verdict with a much larger predictor set. Campbell and Thompson
(2008) argue that even a small positive out-of-sample $R^2$ can be
economically valuable, while Neely, Rapach, Tu and Zhou (2014) report that
technical indicators contain out-of-sample information on their 1966--2011
sample. Machine learning appears to offer a way forward: Gu, Kelly and Xiu
(2020) document gains from shrinkage and nonlinear methods in asset pricing.
Monthly premium forecasting, however, combines a very low signal-to-noise
ratio with many correlated, persistent predictors---exactly the setting in
which flexible models can overfit.

We address three research questions. \emph{RQ1}: can regularized and
dimension-reduction models (or forecast combinations) produce a positive and
statistically significant out-of-sample $R^2$ against the prevailing mean?
\emph{RQ2}: do the Neely et al.\ (2014) technical signals add predictive
value, on their original sample and after 2011? \emph{RQ3}: do the forecasts
deliver economic value to a switching investor or a mean--variance investor
after transaction costs?

Our contribution is a carefully time-aligned comparison rather than a new
model. We rebuild the fourteen technical signals from raw price and volume
data, combine them with lagged macro predictors in a 98-column design,
estimate seven models in a fixed rolling window with horizon-correct
in-window tuning, use the prevailing mean as the only benchmark, and report
both statistical and economic evaluations, including several robustness
checks. Several design choices were revised after the instructor's feedback
on our proposal (Section~\ref{sec:method:changes}).

The answers are largely negative. Only ridge regression has a positive
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ (0.12\% at $h=1$, 0.30\% at $h=3$), no Diebold--Mariano
test is significant at any horizon, and the random forest is the least
accurate model throughout. The single-signal technical regressions are
insignificant on 1966--2011 and negative afterwards. Some switching
strategies end with more wealth than buy-and-hold at $h=1$, but the gains
are model-specific, shrink at longer horizons, and are not supported by a
significance test.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Target and predictors}\label{sec:data:sources}
The target is the monthly \emph{simple} equity premium in percent,
$y_t = 100\,(R^m_t - R^f_t)$, where $R^m_t$ is the S\&P 500 return including
dividends and $R^f_t$ the risk-free rate, both taken from the continuously
updated Goyal--Welch--Zafirov (2024) workbook. Over 1951:02--2025:12 the
premium averages 0.66\% per month with a standard deviation of 4.21\%
(Table~\ref{tab:table1}). The fourteen macroeconomic predictors follow Goyal
and Welch (2008): the dividend--price ratio, dividend yield,
earnings--price ratio, dividend payout ratio, stock variance,
book-to-market, net equity expansion, Treasury bill rate, long-term yield,
long-term return, term spread, default yield spread, default return spread
and inflation. Each enters with lags one to six, giving 84 columns.

The fourteen technical indicators follow Neely et al.\ (2014): six
moving-average rules $\mathrm{MA}(s,l)$ with $s\in\{1,2,3\}$ and
$l\in\{9,12\}$, two momentum rules $\mathrm{MOM}(m)$, $m\in\{9,12\}$, and six
on-balance-volume rules $\mathrm{VOL}(s,l)$. Each is a binary buy signal.
MA and MOM rules use the index level from the Goyal--Welch--Zafirov workbook;
VOL rules use monthly average daily S\&P 500 volume, taken from the Neely et
al.\ replication files for 1950--2011 and from Yahoo Finance for 2012--2025.
Against the published signals over 1950:12--2011:12 (733 months per
signal), twelve signals match exactly and MOM(12) and VOL(2,9) differ in one
month each (1968:03 and 1980:04), an overall match of 99.98\%; we do not
know the cause of the two differences. The baseline design therefore has
98 predictors (84 macro-lag columns and 14 signals).

\subsection{Expanded predictor set}\label{sec:data:expanded}
As a robustness variant we add eight variables from the Goyal et al.\ (2024)
set (skvw, tail, rdsp, avgcor, dtoy, dtoat, wtexas, lzrt), each with six
lags, giving 146 predictors. These are the only additional variables that
are observed monthly throughout the sample and are not flagged as
full-sample constructions. We exclude fbm, ogap, sntm, tchi and shtint
because the data documentation describes them as full-sample constructions,
so lagging them would not remove look-ahead bias; twelve further variables
(e.g.\ cay, pce, eqis) are not available monthly; and csp, vp, impvar, vrp,
ndrbl, ygap, rsvix and disag start after or end before our sample, so
including them would shorten the common sample. The expanded variant is
therefore a partial, not exhaustive, use of the Goyal et al.\ (2024)
predictor set.

\subsection{Alignment and sample}\label{sec:data:align}
Row $t$ of the panel holds the premium earned in month $t$ and predictors
observable at the end of month $t$: macro lag columns satisfy
$x^{(k)}_t = x_{t-k}$, and technical signals use prices and volume through
month $t$ only. The common sample runs from 1951:02 (all lags and signals
available) to 2025:12, giving $T=899$ months. Table~\ref{tab:table1}
reports summary statistics and augmented Dickey--Fuller tests: the premium
is clearly stationary, whereas valuation ratios and interest-rate levels are
highly persistent, which is one reason we rely on regularization and
dimension reduction rather than unrestricted regression.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Forecasting design}\label{sec:method:design}
At the end of month $t$ we forecast the premium earned in month $t+h$ for
$h\in\{1,3,12\}$. The target is always a single month's premium $h$ months
ahead, not a cumulative $h$-month return. A training pair links predictors
$X_s$ with the outcome $y_{s+h}$, and at origin $t$ only pairs with
$s+h\le t$ are eligible, so no outcome after the origin is used. Every
model is re-estimated each month on a fixed window of the 240 most recent
eligible pairs (20 years). The out-of-sample periods contain 658 targets
from 1971:03 at $h=1$, 654 from 1971:07 at $h=3$, and 636 from 1973:01 at
$h=12$. The benchmark is the prevailing mean, the expanding average of all
premia observed up to the origin.

\subsection{Models and tuning}\label{sec:method:models}
The design is wide relative to a 240-month window and its columns are
highly correlated, so we use methods covered in the course that regularize
or compress the predictor space, plus one nonlinear learner. The seven
models are: (i) LASSO with the data-driven plug-in penalty of
\texttt{hdm::rlasso} (Chernozhukov, Hansen and Spindler, 2016), which sets
the penalty from theory and heteroskedasticity-robust loadings instead of
cross-validation; (ii) post-LASSO, an OLS refit on the rlasso-selected
variables; (iii) ridge and (iv) elastic net ($\alpha=0.5$) from
\texttt{glmnet}; (v) principal components regression and (vi) partial
least squares on standardized predictors with at most 20 components (zero
components, i.e.\ the window mean, is also allowed); and (vii) a random
forest with 500 trees, $m_{\mathrm{try}}=\lfloor p/3\rfloor$, minimum node
size 5 and a fixed seed.

The penalty of ridge and elastic net and the number of components of PCR
and PLS are chosen inside each training window by forward chronological
validation. The second half of the window is split into three consecutive
validation blocks; for each block the model is fitted only on earlier pairs
whose outcomes would have been observed when the block starts (the $h-1$
pairs immediately before the block are dropped), and squared validation
errors are summed over the three blocks.
Preprocessing (dropping constant columns, standardization) is redone within
each fold, and the glmnet penalty grid is set from the first training
segment only. The selected tuning parameter is then refitted on the full
window. No information from the test month or later enters any forecast.

\subsection{Forecast combinations}\label{sec:method:combos}
We combine the seven model forecasts with (i) an equal-weight average and
(ii) a constrained Granger--Ramanathan (1984) regression. For the latter,
at each origin the weights solve $\min_w \|y-Fw\|^2$ subject to
$\sum_i w_i=1$ and $w_i\ge0$, where $F$ contains only past out-of-sample
forecasts whose errors are known at that origin. The constraints turn the
regression into a convex weighting that cannot take offsetting long--short
positions in highly correlated forecasts. Until 60 such observations exist,
equal weights are used.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:method:eval}
Accuracy is measured by RMSE, MAE, hit rate and the Campbell--Thompson
(2008) statistic $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}=1-\sum e_t^2/\sum e_{t,\mathrm{PM}}^2$
relative to the prevailing mean. Significance is assessed with one-sided
Diebold--Mariano (1995) tests on squared and absolute error differentials
(alternative: the model is more accurate), using a Newey--West variance with
lag $h-1$ and normal critical values. Following the course, we use the
Diebold--Mariano test only and do not report the Clark--West (2007)
nested-model test.

Economic value is assessed in two ways. First, a binary switching strategy
holds the S\&P 500 in a month if the forecast of that month's premium is
positive and the risk-free asset otherwise; we report terminal wealth per
\$1, the annualized Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown and the number of
switches, and compare with buy-and-hold. In the main specification the
position for month $t$ uses the forecast of month $t$ made at $t-h$, so for
$h>1$ the signal is older; Section~\ref{sec:results:robust} reports an
alternative timing. Every strategy starts in cash and is charged the cost
on every switch, including the initial equity purchase, with no terminal
liquidation. Costs are 10 bps per switch in the baseline, with 5 and 20 bps
as sensitivity cases. We assume the investor trades an S\&P 500 ETF and
Treasury bills; major US brokers now charge zero online commission on both
(e.g.\ Charles Schwab, 2026), and a one-cent quoted spread on a liquid
S\&P 500 ETF priced in the hundreds of dollars is well below 1 bp. The
10 bps baseline is therefore a conservative all-in cost covering spread,
slippage and price impact, and the 20 bps case reflects the higher trading
costs of earlier decades. Second, a mean--variance investor with relative
risk aversion $\gamma=3$ holds $w_t=\hat y_t/(\gamma\hat\sigma^2_t)$ in
equities, bounded to $[0,1.5]$, where $\hat\sigma^2_t$ is the sample
variance of the 60 monthly returns up to the origin. We report the
annualized certainty-equivalent gain $\Delta U$ over the same investor
using the prevailing-mean forecast, net of the same proportional costs on
weight changes.

\subsection{Changes after proposal feedback}\label{sec:method:changes}
Following the instructor's comments on our proposal, we replaced an OLS
benchmark with the prevailing mean, replaced a glmnet LASSO
with rlasso and post-LASSO, replaced the Clark--West test with
Diebold--Mariano tests on squared and absolute errors, extended the macro
predictors from one lag to up to six lags, and added the switching strategy
and the constrained Granger--Ramanathan combination.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Statistical accuracy and significance}\label{sec:results:accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:table2} and Figure~\ref{fig:r2oos} summarize point-forecast
accuracy. Of the 27 model--horizon pairs, only two have a positive
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$: ridge at $h=1$ (0.12\%) and at $h=3$ (0.30\%). At
$h=12$ every model is less accurate than the prevailing mean; the
equal-weight combination comes closest ($-0.48\%$). The random forest is the
least accurate model at every horizon ($-15.53\%$, $-12.33\%$ and
$-10.47\%$), consistent with a flexible learner fitting noise in a
240-month window. The simple combinations are generally close to the
benchmark: the Granger--Ramanathan combination has $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ of
$-0.54\%$ at $h=1$ and the equal-weight combination $-0.14\%$ at $h=3$.

None of these differences is statistically significant. Across nine models,
three horizons and two loss functions (54 tests), no one-sided
Diebold--Mariano test rejects equal accuracy even at 10\%. The smallest
$p$-value is 0.23, for ridge at $h=1$ under absolute-error loss; under
squared-error loss ridge has $p=0.451$ at $h=1$ and $p=0.341$ at $h=3$. The
positive ridge $R^2$ values should therefore be read as indistinguishable
from zero, particularly as they are the best of 27 comparisons. Hit rates
lie between 54.1\% and 61.2\%, against 59.7\% to 59.9\% for the prevailing
mean, so directional accuracy is also close to the benchmark.

Figure~\ref{fig:grweights} shows that the Granger--Ramanathan combination
at $h=1$ concentrates on a few linear models---mainly ridge, PCR and elastic
net, with ridge dominant after 2009---and gives essentially no weight to the
random forest. This reflects which models had smaller past errors, but it
did not translate into a significant gain.

\subsection{Technical indicators: Neely et al.\ (2014) replication and extension}\label{sec:results:neely}
To examine RQ2 directly, we adapt the Neely et al.\ (2014) design:
expanding-window bivariate regressions of the one-month-ahead premium on one
technical signal at a time, compared with the prevailing mean using a
one-sided Diebold--Mariano test on squared errors. Their original design
uses a 181-month initial window; with signals dated before the returns they
predict, our panel provides 178 training pairs before the first forecast in
1966:01. Table~\ref{tab:table5} reports all fourteen signals.

On 1966--2011, ten of the fourteen signals have a positive
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$, the largest being VOL(1,12) at 0.591\%, but none is
significant even at 10\%; the smallest $p$-value is 0.196 for VOL(3,12).
Neely et al.\ (2014) report significant predictability for several of these
indicators using the Clark--West statistic, which is designed for nested
models; our Diebold--Mariano test is more conservative in this setting, so
the two sets of results are not directly comparable. On the 2012--2025
extension all fourteen signals have negative $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$, ranging
from $-0.274\%$ to $-2.535\%$. The same picture holds in our main rolling
design: over 1971--2025, all fourteen single-signal regressions estimated on
the 240-month window have negative $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ (between $-0.527\%$
and $-1.471\%$; smallest $p=0.7543$), and adding the signals to the macro
predictors does not help the multivariate models (Appendix~\ref{app:variants}).

\subsection{Economic evaluation}\label{sec:results:econ}
Table~\ref{tab:table3} reports both strategies and Figure~\ref{fig:wealth}
the switching wealth paths for the model with the highest
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ at each horizon. Because the prevailing mean is
always positive, the prevailing-mean switching strategy is identical to
buy-and-hold, which ends with \$341.59 per dollar at $h=1$ net of 10 bps.
The ridge strategy ends with \$499.36, with a higher Sharpe ratio (0.569
versus 0.494) and a smaller maximum drawdown ($-44.4\%$ versus $-50.2\%$).
The Granger--Ramanathan (\$421.30), PLS (\$398.41) and equal-weight
(\$382.92) strategies also finish above buy-and-hold, and elastic net only
marginally (\$345.52). The other four of the nine strategies finish below
it: PCR (\$330.38), rlasso (\$257.78), post-LASSO (\$242.67) and the random
forest (\$92.58), which switches 167 times and holds equities in only 67.8\%
of months. Costs matter little for slow-switching strategies: ridge falls
from \$508.12 at 5 bps to \$482.26 at 20 bps.

The advantage does not survive longer horizons. At $h=3$ only PLS
(\$505.54), ridge (\$378.79) and post-LASSO (\$336.86) beat buy-and-hold
(\$328.43). At $h=12$ only PCR (\$323.26) ends above buy-and-hold (\$264.75);
post-LASSO never leaves equities and matches it, and the other seven
strategies end below it. Since we run nine strategies at three horizons and
select the best ex post, and since we do not test whether wealth or Sharpe
differences are significant, the $h=1$ gains are suggestive at best. They
also sit uneasily with the insignificant accuracy results: a switching rule
depends only on the sign of the forecast, so its wealth can be driven by
being out of equities in a small number of large down months.

The mean--variance results are similar in spirit. At $h=1$ and 10 bps,
six of nine models give a positive $\Delta U$ relative to the
prevailing-mean investor (whose certainty-equivalent return is 7.16\% p.a.),
led by ridge at 1.92\% p.a. and the Granger--Ramanathan combination at
1.18\%; post-LASSO ($-0.86\%$) and the random forest ($-1.96\%$) lose. At
$h=3$ six models are again positive, and the random forest has the largest
gain (2.21\%) despite the worst $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$, plausibly because
its lower average equity weight (0.761 versus 1.067 for the
prevailing-mean investor) reduces portfolio variance rather than because
its forecasts are more accurate. At
$h=12$ eight of nine models have negative $\Delta U$. These utility gains
are not accompanied by significant accuracy gains and are not themselves
tested.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:results:robust}
\emph{Predictor sets.} Appendix Table~\ref{tab:tableA1} re-estimates the
$h=1$ models with technical signals only (14 columns) and macro lags only
(84 columns). With technical signals only, all nine $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$
values are negative (the best is post-LASSO at $-0.53\%$). With macro lags
only, ridge is again the only positive model (0.17\%). The random forest
improves markedly with the small technical set ($-5.42\%$ versus $-15.53\%$
for the full set), consistent with overfitting in the wide design.

\emph{Expanded predictors.} With the 146-column expanded set, five of 27
model--horizon pairs have positive $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$, the largest being
ridge at $h=3$ (0.847\%), with ridge at $h=1$ only 0.029\%. All models
remain negative at $h=12$ and the random forest remains the worst
($-13.117\%$ at $h=1$). We did not run Diebold--Mariano tests for this
variant, so these values are descriptive only.

\emph{Subperiods.} Table~\ref{tab:subperiods} splits the $h=1$ rlasso
forecasts into three periods. Its $R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ is negative in all
of them and deteriorates over time, from $-0.63\%$ in 1971--1999 to
$-1.20\%$ in 2000--2011 and $-1.94\%$ in 2012--2025. These comparisons are
descriptive and do not identify a cause.

\emph{Switching timing.} For $h>1$, an alternative timing sets each month's
position from the most recent $h$-month-ahead forecast (made in the
previous month), so the evaluation sample shifts by $h-1$ months. Under this
timing, at $h=3$ PLS (\$387.23) and PCR (\$341.14) end above the
prevailing-mean strategy (\$316.02), while ridge (\$278.15) falls below it;
at $h=12$ only PCR (\$310.82) beats the prevailing mean (\$269.73). The
identity of the ``winning'' strategy thus depends on an arbitrary timing
convention, reinforcing that the economic gains are fragile.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
\emph{RQ1.} Regularized machine learning does not reliably beat the
historical average. Ridge regression achieves a small positive
$R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}$ at $h=1$ and $h=3$, but no Diebold--Mariano test is
significant at any horizon, and flexible nonlinear modelling (the random
forest) is clearly harmful. \emph{RQ2.} The technical signals do not add
statistically significant predictive value in our adapted design: on the
1966--2011 sample most single-signal gains are positive but insignificant,
after 2011 all are negative, and technical-only multivariate models are
negative too. \emph{RQ3.} Some forecasts, notably ridge, the
Granger--Ramanathan combination and PLS at $h=1$, produce higher switching
wealth and positive certainty-equivalent gains net of costs, but these
gains depend on model choice, horizon and timing convention, fade at
$h=12$, and are not tested for significance. Our overall conclusion is that
the prevailing mean remains a hard benchmark to beat.

The study has several limitations. We use the latest data vintage rather
than real-time data, so macro predictors may contain revisions unavailable
to investors at the time. Our technical signals match the published series
in 99.98\% of signal-months, not perfectly, and the 2012--2025 volume
series is spliced from a different source. The Neely et al.\ (2014)
analysis is an adapted, not exact, replication (different initial window,
Diebold--Mariano rather than Clark--West inference). With nine forecasts,
three horizons and several robustness variants, the few positive results
are exactly what multiple comparisons would produce by chance, and we do
not correct for this selection. The economic gains carry no significance
test, and the expanded predictor set uses only eight of the Goyal et al.\
(2024) variables. Future work could apply formal tests of economic value,
real-time vintages, and shrinkage of model forecasts towards the prevailing
mean.
